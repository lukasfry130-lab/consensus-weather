How to get a GPX file from Strava
  1. On strava.com, open your route (Dashboard → My Routes) or a past ride.
  2. Open the route's ⋯ menu and choose Export GPX. For a ride, it's ⋯ → Export GPX.
  3. Drop the downloaded file here. You can also paste a Strava link here and you'll get a direct download link.

Strava won't let other websites read routes straight from a link, so the file is needed.

      How the ride forecast works

      The route is split into checkpoints about every 10 km (6 miles). For each one, the app asks up to nine forecast models what the weather will be at the time you'll get there, and blends them the same way as the main forecast.

      Arrival times come from your average speed, slowed on climbs and a little faster on descents, with no stops. Long cafe stops will push everything later.

      Headwind or tailwind compares the blended wind direction with the direction you're riding on every stretch of road. Wind within 45° of straight ahead is a headwind, within 45° of straight behind is a tailwind, and anything else is a crosswind. Forecast wind is measured 10 m up. At handlebar height it's usually about a third lighter, and trees and buildings block more.

      Feels at speed adds the wind you make by riding, plus any headwind, to the wind chill. That's why it reads colder than the thermometer, and it's what the clothing advice uses.

      How the consensus is built

      Instead of copying other websites, the app goes to the same sources those websites use: the raw output of the world's main forecast models, plus the US National Weather Service's forecaster-edited forecast and MET Norway's forecast.

      Temperatures, wind and amounts use a weighted average with the highest and lowest values dropped. In skill-weighted mode, models with stronger records in global accuracy comparisons (ECMWF first) count for more. MET Norway is built largely on ECMWF data, so it gets a lower weight to avoid counting the same model twice.

      Chance of precipitation is the weighted share of sources that predict measurable precipitation (at least 0.01 in). The NWS adds its own stated probability. This works like an ensemble forecast: if 7 of 9 models show rain, rain is likely.

      Wind arrows point the way the wind is blowing toward, so ride in the arrow's direction for a tailwind and against it for a headwind. Forecasts report where wind comes from, and that's shown in words too. Directions are averaged as vectors. "Shifty" means the sources disagree on the direction.

      Extended outlook (days 8–16) adds ensemble forecasts. ECMWF, NOAA and Canada each re-run their model 20–50 times with slightly different starting conditions. The ensemble average is a better long-range guess than any single run, so it gets extra weight. The spread of all the runs gives the "likely" range. Precipitation chance is the share of runs that turn wet. Confidence drops as the runs spread apart.

      Agreement shows how far apart the sources' daily highs are. A wide spread means the forecast really is uncertain, which is worth knowing before a ride or an event.
